EU2050 reads EU policy and news every day to track two futures for Europe. Scenario A is a Union where integration is conditioned under national vetoes. Scenario B is a genuine federation — one Single Market, one European Capital Market, European Digital Sovereignty, and a completed enlargement.

SCENARIO A — Status quo
A fragmented Union, 2050
  • Unanimity rules stall the single market.
  • Capital markets stay split along national lines, and digital infrastructure depends on US and Chinese platforms.
  • Enlargement happens unevenly, leaving the EU a price-taker on rules written by Washington and Beijing.

CO2 emissions
2,800 Mt
2026 baseline: ~2,800 Mt CO2e/year (EEA). The EU's legally binding target of net-zero emissions by 2050 — the European Climate Law, Regulation (EU) 2021/1119 — commits the whole bloc to climate neutrality, but under this scenario policy fragmentation falls short — landing around 1,100 Mt by 2050, well short of the target. Under this scenario the target isn't simply missed but progressively dismantled: without a unified industrial and carbon-border strategy, individual capitals win sector-by-sector exemptions under competitiveness pressure, the ETS2 rollout is repeatedly delayed, the 2040 interim target is renegotiated downward, and by the 2040s the European Climate Law's 2050 deadline is treated as aspirational rather than binding, with the original legal commitment eroded through amendment and non-enforcement rather than ever being formally repealed. For comparison, 2026 baselines: US ~4,900 Mt (targeting net zero by 2050, contested), China ~12,300 Mt (targeting peak before 2030, neutrality by 2060 — not 2050). Sources: EEA, Global Carbon Project, IEA, European Climate Law (Regulation (EU) 2021/1119).

    Global GDP market share
    18%
    2026 baseline: EU 18% of world GDP (IMF/World Bank; ~$21T of ~$118T nominal). By 2050 (illustrative): EU Federation: 19%, US: ~23%, China: ~17%. Coordinated industrial policy (the EU Chips Act, Net-Zero Industry Act and Critical Raw Materials Act) lifts manufacturing's share of EU GDP from roughly 15% today to an estimated 21% by 2050, reshoring high-value production — semiconductors, batteries, clean-tech machinery — that today is largely imported. That reshoring, combined with enlargement's larger economic base, narrows the EU's goods trade deficit with China from roughly $1bn/day (€359.8bn in 2025, Eurostat) to an estimated $400-450m/day by 2050 — the EU produces more of what it currently buys, even as large-scale trade with China continues in categories where China retains a durable cost advantage. Sources: IMF, World Bank, Eurostat trade statistics.

Sources: OECD, industry reports.
